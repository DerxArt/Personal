Don't use em-dashes (—) or arrow connectors (→) anywhere in CVs or cover
letters, even sparingly. Write like it was typed by hand, not generated —
plain punctuation, ordinary connecting words ("from X to Y" instead of
"X → Y").

Cover letters should sound like Artur's LinkedIn voice: friendly, warm,
a little playful, not corporate and not full of pathos ("I would be
extremely honored to..."). No inflated enthusiasm, no stiff formal
register.

**Preferred cover letter structure** (his own words, paraphrased):
1. Genuine, casual opening — real interest in the company/role, said
   plainly, not "I am writing to express my interest."
2. Brief "here's what I did" — a couple of concrete things, no résumé
   restatement.
3. "How can I help you?" — framed as genuinely wanting to solve their
   problem, not selling himself.
4. Map his experience directly onto the pains/requirements the job post
   named: "I see you need X — I've done that because Y" — concrete,
   not a generic skills list.

**Why:** He gave this feedback right after I drafted a cover letter in a
more neutral/formal register with em-dashes — he wants every outward-facing
document from this project to read as obviously written by a person, in
his own voice, not AI-polished corporate prose.

**Update 2026-09-09, after he called a draft "slop":** "просто я й слоп"
("just me and slop") was his verdict on a cover letter that hit the right
structural beats but read like nobody in particular wrote it. He wants
letters people actually notice: unordinary, a little strange, opinionated,
funny, willing to admit a mistake. Read his real LinkedIn
(linkedin.com/in/artur-derda-a791812a6/recent-activity/all/) before writing
another one — his actual posts are the calibration, not a description of
them. Concrete patterns his posts use, worth reusing in cover letters:

- **Cold open with a real, slightly uncomfortable scene**, not a claim.
  Example: "У моєму CV п'ять різних посад за три роки. Якийсь час мене це
  бентежило." (starts with something that could look like a weakness, then
  reframes it.) Another: describes lying awake at 2am re-googling
  productivity apps he'd already installed three of, before making the
  actual point about tool-switching.
- **Chronological build-up as a list of concrete, named things**, not a
  skills summary. His "five jobs in three years" post walks Żabka, then
  Wallpanels, then Otake-PM, then Otake-growth, then HabitForest, each in
  one or two plain sentences, before naming the pattern that connects them
  ("в кожній з цих п'яти ролей я заходив туди, де процесу ще нема або він
  розвалюється").
- **States the real constraint out loud instead of hiding it.** "Чесно,
  зараз немає доходу, і це підганяє. Але не настільки, щоб брати
  першу-ліпшу вакансію" — he names the pressure and the boundary in the
  same breath. Don't sand this off into generic enthusiasm.
- **Ends with a real, specific invitation**, addressed to the actual
  reader, not a generic CTA. His job-search post ends: "Якщо у вашій
  команді саме зараз щось на межі розвалу, а процесу ще нема, можливо, нам
  варто поговорити." A cover letter should close the same way: something
  that only makes sense for this company, not swappable boilerplate.
- **First person, plain Ukrainian-register English when he writes in
  English** — short declarative sentences mixed with one longer one,
  occasional sentence fragments for emphasis. Not polished corporate
  cadence even at the sentence-rhythm level.

**How this changes cover-letter writing:** don't just avoid banned words
and em-dashes, actually build the letter as a small story with one real
scene or admission in it, using his specific documented cases (Żabka,
Wallpanels $400K, Otake's blank Jira, HabitForest), and end with a
pointed, company-specific invitation instead of "would love to chat."
