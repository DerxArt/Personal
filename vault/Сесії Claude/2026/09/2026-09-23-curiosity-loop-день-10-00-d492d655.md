## Claude

Good question — let's untangle it, because "give them a choice" and "Zeigarnik" aren't automatically the same thing.

Choice alone just makes people feel involved — that's more of an IKEA-effect thing, "I helped build it, so I care about it." Zeigarnik is specifically about *incompleteness* sticking in memory. The trick is: when you hand a stakeholder an unresolved decision — "here are two directions, we haven't locked one in yet" — you're planting an open task in their brain. Their mind literally keeps circling back to it between meetings, because unfinished things nag at us more than finished ones. If you'd already picked for them and handed over a polished, closed deliverable, there's nothing left to chew on — out of sight, out of mind until the next call.

So the choice is just the *mechanism* you use to create the open loop. You could create the same effect without a choice at all — e.g. "I have a strong instinct on how to solve this, but I want to test it before I commit" — that's also unresolved, also nagging.

Let's actually test this on you. Pitch me a real (or made-up) project update, as if I'm your slightly impatient stakeholder — and end it deliberately unresolved, on purpose leaving me hanging on something. I'll react and push back like a real client would.
